01 — The Problem

Why Build This?

The core problem: too much information, complex logic, and it takes humans months to learn how to "spot trouble at a glance" when reading financial reports.

Too Much Information

A single annual report can be 200+ pages. Balance sheets, income statements, cash flow statements, footnotes — every number could hide accounting tricks. Reading it all takes days or even weeks, and it's easy to miss critical red flags.

Logic Is Complicated

Receivables vs. revenue growth, profit margins vs. industry average, cash flow vs. net profit — you need to cross-check multiple tables and understand accounting rules. It's not a simple checklist; it's a chain of reasoning.

Takes Too Long to Learn

It took the investing mentor 20 years to build his "problem checklist". Even after reading his books, a normal person needs dozens of practice reports to build intuition. Can AI do 80% of this grunt work for me?

02 — Architecture

How Does It Work?

Three layers: Data (chop the books into pieces), Logic (store rules so AI can search them), Interaction (let AI search the knowledge base before answering).

Layer 1: Data Chopping the Books

Book → Small pieces → Save & search

I took two investing books and chopped them into 1,139 small knowledge pieces. Each piece keeps its chapter context, judgment criteria, and case examples. I also tagged every piece with labels like "which accounting item it covers" and "how serious the red flag is".

Layer 3: Interaction RAG — Search Before Speak

The AI must search the knowledge base FIRST, then answer — no making things up

I use a technique called RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation). It means: every time the AI makes a judgment, it must search the knowledge base first, then reason based on what it finds — instead of making up answers from its own training data. This eliminates AI's biggest flaw: hallucination (confidently saying wrong things).

03 — Features

What Can It Do?

An automated "6-step checking pipeline" + automatic "red flag scanner" — an auditor that never gets lazy, never skips steps, and doesn't play favorites.

Step 1

Read the Basics

Data
Extract company name, industry, reporting period, stock exchange, and auditor's opinion. Figure out which market rules apply — the Agent supports China A-shares, Hong Kong stocks, and US stocks, each with its own accounting standards and rule set.
Step 2

Calculate Key Numbers

Calculate
Use a calculator tool to independently compute: profit margin, net profit ratio, expense ratio, cash flow vs. net profit, debt ratio, ROE, receivables growth vs. revenue growth, inventory growth vs. revenue growth. Every number is calculated from scratch — never copied from the report.
Step 3

Check Each Item One by One

Search
For each of 17 key accounting items (receivables, inventory, goodwill, fixed assets, investments, construction in progress, R&D spending, etc.), search the knowledge base and compare against the expert's standards.
Step 4

Sort Problems by Severity

Judge
Classify all findings into three levels: Red — hard red flag (immediate reject), Yellow — needs more checking, Green — looks fine. Every conclusion must cite a specific rule from the knowledge base.
Step 5

Overall Judgment

Judge
Ask the three fundamental questions: Are profits real? Are they sustainable? Can the company maintain them without spending huge amounts of capital? Then give a final yes/no verdict.
Step 6

Generate Report

Output
Produce a complete HTML audit report with: summary dashboard, key metrics, item-by-item scan results, traffic-light verdicts, and overall conclusion. One report, everything clear at a glance.

Automatic Red Flag Scanner

The most critical "hard red flag" rules from my 22-rule set — if ANY one of these triggers, the Agent flashes a red light and recommends avoiding the stock. Here are some key examples:

Receivables growing faster than revenue — customers might not be paying
Cash flow from operations below net profit for 3 years — profits might not be real cash
Profit margin way off from industry average — needs a really good explanation
Inventory growing faster than revenue — products might not be selling
Goodwill over 30% of net assets — huge write-down risk
Construction in progress not turning into assets for years — might be hiding expenses
Too many related-party transactions — money might be flowing to friends
Auditor's opinion is not "clean" — the auditors themselves see problems
04 — Value

Why Does This Matter Beyond Finance?

The real value isn't just "catching bad financial reports" — it proves a universal pattern for building AI agents in any rule-heavy, cross-checking scenario.

Same Architecture, Different Industries

The same "chop expert knowledge → store in searchable database → force AI to follow a pipeline" approach can be applied to ANY field with lots of rules and cross-checking:

  • Airline safety compliance — chop aviation authority rules into knowledge pieces, automatically check if an airline's operations comply
  • Hospital shift scheduling — encode labor laws and union contracts as rules, automatically audit if a schedule violates them
  • Airport slot coordination — put IATA scheduling rules into the database, help coordinators make better decisions
  • Ferry and train dispatching — encode safety rules and working-hour limits, auto-check if a schedule is legal
  • Any "expert judgment → encode as rules → AI automates it" scenario

Every Conclusion Has a Source
Fully traceable

Every audit finding cites a specific rule number and quotes from the knowledge base. A human auditor can trace every step of the AI's reasoning — it's transparent, not a black box.

Zero Hallucination
No making things up

The RAG architecture means the AI can only say what's in the knowledge base. "The database is its brain, the pipeline is its discipline, the calculator is its hands" — all three are essential.

Tested on Real Markets
Both good and bad companies

Successfully tested on real stocks: correctly identified a Chinese A-share company (Guangdao Digital — 99% fake revenue, later delisted) as a fraud, and correctly judged Tencent (Hong Kong) as a healthy company. It doesn't falsely accuse the good guys, and doesn't let the bad guys slip through.
